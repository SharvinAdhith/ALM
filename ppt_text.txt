Problem Statement(s)
Define the Problem: 
Existing AI systems process speech and non-speech audio in isolation - no single model unifies both.
Current systems lack contextual audio reasoning - they cannot infer real-world situations from combined sound and speech signals.
 Emotion is detected from text only, not from actual voice tone - missing a critical human communication channel (paralinguistics).
There is no unified model that understands both what is said and what is happening in the background.

Context and Background: 
In the 6th semester, we built JARVIS - a voice assistant that listens and responds. 
However, it only understood spoken words (speech). It could not detect background sounds like rain, engines, or crowd noise. 
Research models like SALMONN and Qwen-Audio explore joint audio understanding, but remain research-only demos with no deployable full-stack implementation. 
The voice AI market ($30B+) is rapidly growing, yet no commercial assistant - Alexa, Siri, or Google - supports non-speech audio understanding alongside reasoning.
In 7th semester, we upgrade it to understand the full audio scene.

Literature Survey - Introduction
Domain Overview:
Audio Language Models (ALMs) are deep-learning systems that jointly process speech, non-speech sound events and natural language so that a single model can transcribe, classify and reason about an audio scene.
They combine self-supervised audio encoders (e.g., Whisper, BEATs, CLAP) with Large Language Models (LLMs) through adapters, Q-Formers or LoRA fine-tuning.
Recent IEEE ICASSP/TASLP work (2024-2026) has pushed ALMs from narrow ASR/classification tools toward multi-task audio captioning, reasoning, emotion-aware and instruction-following systems.
This survey grounds the JARVIS ALM 2.0 project - which unifies speech transcription, non-speech sound classification and voice-emotion detection.

Survey Scope:
15 peer-reviewed IEEE papers were reviewed, drawn from IEEE ICASSP (2024-2026) and IEEE/ACM Transactions on Audio, Speech and Language Processing.
Selection criteria: (1) published by IEEE, (2) directly related to audio-language modelling, audio captioning, speech emotion recognition or audio-text representation learning, (3) 2023-2026 publication window, weighted toward 2024-2026.

Comparative Analysis of Existing Approaches
Advantages of Existing Approaches:
Strong single-task performance: Whisper/CLAP-style encoders and ICASSP-2024/25 captioning models (EnCLAP, DRCap, SLAM-AAC) reach high accuracy on ASR and audio captioning.
Reusable pretraining: contrastive audio-text embeddings (CLAP, Cacophony) transfer well to captioning, retrieval and zero-shot classification.
Rich benchmarking: Dynamic-SUPERB and the ICASSP 2026 HumDial Challenge provide standardised, multi-task evaluation of audio-LLMs.
Data-efficient adaptation: LLM-assisted annotation (Santoso et al.; Feng & Narayanan) cuts manual-labelling cost for emotion recognition.

Limitations of Existing Approaches:
Task-specific silos: most models (captioning, ASR, SER, quality evaluation) are trained and evaluated on a single task, not a unified speech + non-speech + emotion pipeline.
Hallucination and unreliable reasoning: Kuan & Lee (2025) show current large audio-language models frequently hallucinate about audio content that was never present.
Research-scale only: most systems (captioning/quality models, HumDial baselines) are benchmarked offline and are not packaged as deployable, real-time full-stack applications.
No persistent memory or agentic tool use is combined with joint speech + sound-event + emotion understanding in any single reviewed system.
Research Gap and Justification
Identified Research Gap:
No reviewed IEEE paper unifies speech transcription, non-speech sound-event classification and voice-emotion recognition inside one deployable, real-time system.
Hallucination and reasoning failures (Kuan & Lee, 2025) remain unresolved for open-domain audio question answering.
Existing captioning/SER/quality-assessment models are evaluated as standalone research artefacts, not integrated with persistent memory (RAG) or agentic tool use.
Benchmarks such as Dynamic-SUPERB and HumDial evaluate models offline; none provide a live, streaming, full-stack assistant.

Justification for Proposed Project:
JARVIS ALM 2.0 fuses Whisper (speech), a PANNs/BEATs-style classifier (non-speech events) and Wav2Vec 2.0 (voice emotion) - directly addressing the task-silo gap found across papers 1-15.
A persistent-memory RAG layer plus agentic tools move beyond the offline-benchmark scope of Dynamic-SUPERB/HumDial toward a deployable assistant.
Stepwise reasoning and multi-signal grounding (speech + sound + tone) are used to reduce the hallucination risk reported by Kuan & Lee (2025).
The project therefore extends current IEEE research from isolated audio-understanding components to one full-stack, real-time Audio Language Model.
Existing Technology 
Current Solutions: 
OpenAI Whisper handles speech-to-text. 
PANNs (CNN14) classifies 527 environmental sounds. SpeechBrain + Wav2Vec 2.0 detects emotion from speech audio. 
ChromaDB enables vector-based semantic memory.
 Azure OpenAI GPT provides reasoning and response generation.

Limitations: 
Each model works in isolation. 
Whisper cannot understand background sounds. 
PANNs cannot process speech. 
Emotion models only analyze text, not actual voice tone. 
No existing tool combines all three into one unified, reasoning system.

Proposed Work/ Methodology
Concept and Approach: 
We take raw audio input and run it through three parallel AI modules: 
Whisper for speech transcription. 
PANNs/BEATs for non-speech sound classification (527 categories). 
Wav2Vec 2.0 for voice emotion detection. The combined results are fed into GPT with memory (RAG) to generate intelligent, context-aware answers.

Innovations: 
Our innovation is the joint audio pipeline: 
No existing deployable system merges real-time speech, sound event, and emotion recognition      	with persistent memory and agentic tools in one full-stack web application. 
We also add SSE token streaming for real-time word-by-word responses.
